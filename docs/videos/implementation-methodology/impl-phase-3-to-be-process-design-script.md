# Video script: Phase 3 — TO-BE Process Design

Narrated lesson video for ERP Implementation Methodology. Video file: public/videos/impl-phase-3-to-be-process-design.mp4. Voice: en-IN-NeerjaNeural.

| # | On screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title card: **Phase 3 — TO-BE Process Design**. The bridge between signed requirements and configuration. | Welcome to lesson four. In this video you will learn how to design to-be processes, map every step to an E R P app, document and role, place approvals sensibly, and record fit-gap decisions before configuration starts. |
| 2 | How will the business work in the future? | Maple Leaf Office Interiors, a fictional company, designs and fits out offices. Today a designer quotes in a spreadsheet, the owner approves every quote by email, a buyer re-types items into vendor emails, and installation is planned on a whiteboard. Requirements are now signed off, so the question becomes how the business will work in the future using the E R P. That future state is the to-be design, and it is the bridge between requirements and configuration. |
| 3 | **Improve the process, don't copy it**. Cards: Start from AS-IS + RTM · Remove waste · Prefer standard · Add controls · Walk it in a prototype | Design in five steps. Start from the as-is and the requirement matrix. Remove the steps that only existed because systems were disconnected, like re-typing and manual reconciliation. Prefer the E R P's standard flow, and change the process where reasonable. Add controls at the right points. Finally, walk the flow with key users in a prototype, because people understand a flow when they click through it, not when they read it. |
| 4 | **Every step has an app**. Animated flow: Quotation ▸ Order confirmed ▸ Advance invoice ▸ POs per vendor ▸ Receive & deliver ▸ Installation ▸ Final invoice & payment | Here is Maple Leaf's to-be flow. The designer creates the quotation in Sales, and only a discount above ten percent goes to the owner. Once the customer confirms, Accounting issues an advance invoice, and Purchase creates orders per vendor. Inventory receives the goods and delivers them to site. Installation tasks run in Project, or in Field Service, which is an Enterprise app. The flow ends with the final invoice, payment and reconciliation. |
| 5 | Step → app, document, role, scope | Every step in the flow is then mapped to an app, a document, a role and a scope label. The scope label is important. It tells everyone whether the step is standard, needs configuration, or may need customisation. Discount approval, for example, is marked configuration or customisation, to be verified in the prototype, because the available approval options depend on your instance. |
| 6 | Controls only where the risk is | The approval matrix defines each control, its rule and who approves. At Maple Leaf, sales discounts above ten percent go to the owner, purchase orders over five thousand go to the operations manager, and customers overdue by more than thirty days go to the finance manager. Some controls need no approver at all, like making the project and delivery address mandatory. Design approvals deliberately and sparingly, because every extra level slows the business down. |
| 7 | Each gap gets options and a decision | The fit-gap analysis records, for each requirement, the standard behaviour, the fit level, the options considered and the decision. Profit per project is a fit, solved with analytic accounts. The branded installation sign-off sheet is a gap, and the decision is a custom report. Discount approval is partial, so the team will prototype both options and choose in the conference room pilot. |
| 8 | Step 85%: **Three approval levels? Check the data** (who, Odoo menu, impact) with an animated **TB-O2C-01 Quote to Install** card | Maple Leaf's owner first wanted three approval levels on every quotation. Configuring that as requested would have been a mistake. In a conference room pilot, the owner saw that eighty five percent of quotations had no discount above ten percent. So approval was limited to high discount quotations only, saving about a day per quote. The lesson is to check the data, then put a control only where the risk is. |
| 9 | **Common design pitfalls**. Cards: Paving the cow path · Paper-only design · Too many approvals · Forgotten exceptions · Configuring before sign-off | Avoid these pitfalls. Paving the cow path means copying the old process into the new system instead of improving it. Designing only on paper. Adding too many approval levels. Forgetting exceptions such as returns, cancellations and partial receipts. And starting configuration before design sign-off, which almost always means redoing work. Sign-off by process owners is what protects scope and budget. |
| 10 | **Key takeaways**. Cards: TO-BE = the future · Map every step · Controls sparingly · Fit-gap + sign-off | To sum up. The to-be design defines how the business will run on the E R P, step by step. Map every step to an app, document, role and scope label. Design approvals deliberately and sparingly. Record each gap's options and decision in the fit-gap, and get sign-off before configuration. Now try the exercises in this lesson, starting with the as-is versus to-be comparison for Maple Leaf. |
