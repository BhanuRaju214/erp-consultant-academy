# Video script: Implementation Approaches: Waterfall, Agile and Hybrid

Narrated lesson video for ERP Implementation Methodology. Video file: public/videos/impl-implementation-approaches.mp4. Voice: en-IN-NeerjaNeural.

| # | On screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title card: **Waterfall, Agile and Hybrid**. Choosing an agreed way to move from "we signed" to "the business runs on the new system". | Welcome to the first lesson of E R P Implementation Methodology. In this video you will compare the three main ways of running an E R P project, waterfall, agile and hybrid, and learn how to pick the right one for a client. |
| 2 | **Same contract week, different needs**. Cards: Atlas Precision Engineering · BrightPath Learning Services | Two fictional clients sign E R P contracts in the same week. Atlas Precision Engineering needs a fixed price and a fixed go-live date approved by its board. BrightPath Learning Services wants to use C R M and invoicing within six weeks, and figure out the rest as they go. Both need a methodology, but clearly not the same one. |
| 3 | **A methodology gives predictability**. Cards: Clear next steps · Checkpoints · Control · Reuse | A methodology is simply an agreed way of getting from signing the contract to the business running on the new system, with predictable quality. Everyone knows what happens next. Deliverables and sign-offs act as checkpoints, so problems surface early instead of at go-live. Scope, time and cost share a common vocabulary. And templates and checklists can be reused on the next project. |
| 4 | **Each phase is signed off before the next starts**. Animated flow: Discovery ▸ Requirements ▸ Design ▸ Build / config ▸ Test ▸ Go-live ▸ Support | In waterfall, phases follow one another, and each ends with a sign-off before the next begins. That gives clear contracts and budgets and strong documentation, which suits regulated industries and fixed price deals. The weakness is that users see the system late. Once the design is signed, changes become expensive, and the business waits a long time for its first value. |
| 5 | **Short sprints, continuous re-prioritisation**. Cards: Strengths · Weaknesses | Agile splits the work into short sprints, often two to three weeks. Each sprint configures and demonstrates a slice of the system, and the backlog is re-prioritised continuously. Users get early feedback and shape the system by seeing it, which is great when requirements are unclear. But it is harder to fix price and scope, it needs a client product owner who is available every week, and because E R P modules are tightly integrated, small slices can be tricky. |
| 6 | Comparing the three approaches | Most E R P partners use a hybrid. They keep waterfall style governance, with gated discovery, design sign-off, cutover and go-live, but run iterative build cycles inside configuration and development. Instead of designing everything on paper, consultants configure a prototype early and run conference room pilots, where key users try real scenarios and the team refines. |
| 7 | Step 3 wk: **A hybrid in practice** (who, Odoo menu, impact) with an animated **Atlas project plan** card | Atlas used a hybrid. A four week discovery and requirements phase ended with a signed requirement traceability matrix and a fixed budget. Configuration and development then ran in three week cycles, each ending with a key user demo. A formal user acceptance test and a go or no-go gate came before cutover. BrightPath, meanwhile, went lighter and more agile: C R M and invoicing went live in week six, and projects and timesheets followed in two later sprints. |
| 8 | **Nine phases, nine named deliverables**. Cards: 1 Discovery · 2 Requirements · 3 TO-BE design · 4 Configuration · 5 Data migration · 6 Development · 7 Testing & training · 8 Go-live · 9 Support | This course follows nine phases, and each one produces a named deliverable, so every phase has a clear finish line and something concrete to sign off. Discovery produces the as-is document, requirements produce the traceability matrix, and design produces the to-be document with a signed fit-gap. Phases four to seven often overlap in iterations, while phases one to three and go-live are usually gated by formal sign-off. |
| 9 | **Ask three questions, then choose**. Cards: Fixed price and date? · Stable requirements? · Weekly product owner? | When choosing, ask three questions. Does the client need a fixed price and date approved up front? Are requirements well understood and stable? And is there a product owner available every week? A common case is a board that insists on a fixed budget, while key users cannot describe their future processes until they see the system. The answer is hybrid. For Odoo, most partners also favour a standard-first, iterative approach, demonstrating standard flows early and keeping customizations minimal. |
| 10 | **Key takeaways**. Cards: Methodology = predictability · Fit the approach to the client · Prototype with key users · Nine phases, nine deliverables | To sum up. A methodology gives predictable steps, deliverables and sign-offs. Waterfall suits fixed scope and price, agile suits evolving scope, and hybrid suits most E R P projects. Iterative prototyping with key users catches design problems before user acceptance testing. Now try the exercises in this lesson, starting with the decision tree and putting the nine phases in order. |
